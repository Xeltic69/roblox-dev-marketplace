# Team, production and operating mindset (creator field notes)

Practitioner heuristics from one producer's videos (see [sources-attribution.md](sources-attribution.md)); useful when the user asks about project management, hiring, scope or how to run a game business. They are one team's experience, not universal rules. Do not give personalized financial advice.

## Idea vs. execution
Ideas are cheap; execution is the bottleneck, and the hardest part of execution is talent. Rough split offered: 80% idea and execution (market research, fantasy, simplicity, depth, mobile/console, mass appeal), 20% optimization of stats with data ([metrics](platform-discovery-metrics.md)). Learn what "the business behind the business" is: here, acquiring and managing talent.

## Roles
A producer/designer (non-coder) can partner with a solo dev: dev = artist, producer = reads data, suggests changes, handles monetization, marketing, project management. Pair a producer with a dev who can execute quickly.

## Hiring
- Hire where the community is: Twitter/X (post what you are building; hire same hour at times), YouTube. Build a reputation: a hit game or building in public; top devs hire people with many mutual connections.
- Prefer **T-shaped** people (programmer who also does UI/animation, builder who also models). Run lean: a 2-4 person team beats a big one.
- **Test before hiring**: ask for a sample (a model) or a small paid task. Hire fast, fire faster.
- Same time zone, so work flows and people motivate each other.
- Contracts: always sign one. Paid upon completion preferred (trust-dependent), or 50% upfront; never pay in full upfront. Do not work with someone who refuses a contract.
- Statement of work: agree upfront on everything to deliver; avoid scope creep; new scope = new agreement.
- Pay on time and respect people: their livelihoods depend on your word.
- Avoid bought templates (they teach little and rank poorly), unvetted hires through marketplaces, and large studios that treat your game as a number (but a big studio can help; use judgment).
- Standards: you set the standard. Missed promises ("25 models by Wednesday, 4 delivered"), excuses, drifting culture → part ways with pay for completed work. One bad apple poisons the team.
- Give an **art style guide and references** (AI-generated mood boards) from the start: otherwise you get three art styles from eight modelers. Unclear output is usually unclear direction.
- Use Loom videos, pictures and links to reference games to communicate.

## Phases of a game build (producer's process)
1. Market research and idea ([ideation](ideation-market-validation.md)).
2. Game design document: what the game is, what players do, how they level up, economy, progression. It doubles as statement of work; an AI can extract "what the programmer must do".
3. Task board (Trello) with columns per discipline; chunk the GDD into actionable cards with explicit details and reference links.
4. Optional: a quick AI prototype (basic parts and free models) to validate fun before hiring.
5. Phase one: models, builds and UI for the core loop first, **before** the programmer works (a built world motivates programmers).
6. Phase two: programmer brings the loop to life; MVP = core loop + some progression.
7. Playtest; if it is good enough, launch, start ads, read feedback; if not, copy a backup, prototype tweaks with AI, then go back to the programmer after 2-3 iteration cycles.
Overthinking before launch is a common mistake; shipping gives data.

## AI usage
- AI prototyping for a working loop before hiring; AI to get progression ideas; AI to speed up thumbnails; AI to summarize player feedback.
- AI-generated models were delivered by two contractors and were badly rigged: verify rigs and animation quality.

## Mindset and discipline (producer's opinions)
Discipline over motivation; do what you say; respect people and pay on time; focus on yourself not other people's "low-effort" success; no one owes you help, so offer value when networking (a consultation payment, a bug report, a played game); take accountability (algorithm, devs ghosting, saturation are reasons to reflect and improve); volume of work (test many thumbnails, play your game, read feedback) wins; lock in on one stat; be patient with data; ask whether you would still make games with unlimited money; passion creates effortless energy. Treat these as perspective, not requirements, and do not push them on the user.

## Be honest about outcomes
Reported revenue figures in creator videos are often extrapolated from a short window and later fall short, and trend simulators can have lifespans of about a month; both show survivorship and extrapolation. Plan longevity and economy health ([iteration](iteration-liveops-playtesting.md)).

## Cost of learning and minors
The creator reports spending thousands of dollars on early games that never launched or never earned back their cost (a contractor-built flop, a bought template, a salaried small team): plan scope and budget accordingly. Many Roblox contractors are minors: use platform-compliant payment, never request personal data, and involve guardians where required.
