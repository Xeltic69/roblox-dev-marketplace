# Ideation and market validation (creator field notes)

Practitioner heuristics from one producer's videos, including a checklist the creator reports from a paid consultation with a top developer (see [sources-attribution.md](sources-attribution.md); complements [market-research.md](market-research.md), which sets the evidence rules). None of this guarantees success; ideas are cheap, execution is the bottleneck.

## Where to find ideas that already have demand

Look for **signals of demand**: a quantifiable number showing people want the core loop, ideally years ago (so the demand is proven and nobody modernized it).

| Source | What to look at |
| --- | --- |
| Old flash/browser game sites (Addicting Games, Coolmath Games, Crazy Games, Newgrounds) | Ratings/votes (e.g. 3M ratings, 85-200K votes), top lists. Crazy Games hosts Unity (3D) games, easiest to translate. Check whether YouTubers played it (6.2M views years ago is a demand signal) |
| Steam / Xbox Game Pass | Wishlists (e.g. 200K for an unreleased airport-security game), sales (10M), creator content. Younger players who cannot afford or run the PC game look for a Roblox version. Watch for comments like "where can I play this? I don't have a PC" |
| Mobile stores (iOS/Google Play) | Download counts (50M); viral casual mechanics |
| TikTok challenges | Easy mini-games from viral challenges (bomb click 260M visits, lying challenge 78M, rubber band watermelon ~20M). Low development time. Considered an untapped source |
| Minecraft mini-games / Garry's Mod modes | Bedwars, spleef; Prop Hunt became Hide or Die (changed to 6v6) |
| Roblox itself, via RoTrends and Creator Exchange | See below |
| Your own game's core players | Their Discord server tags and "what did you play before?" answers reveal other source games (one led to a 2018 base-raiding game with 92M visits) |

When adapting an adult game, a real skill is dummying down the mechanics to be readable to a young audience.

## Finding Roblox games before they blow up (RoTrends method)
- Sort "top moving games" by visits/momentum. Look for **high CCU relative to visits**, recently released (ideally this month), easy to develop (obby, simulator, tycoon, quiz, clicker).
- Open the 90-day CCU graph. Treat it like a stock chart: enter when trending up before the peak (e.g. around the first spike).
- Prefer a small game with a good core loop whose **developer does not update** and whose community lists many bugs. Check update cadence. That is a gap: strong loop, weak execution.
- Avoid copying whatever tops the front page right now; copies die in under a month. One creator reports modest passive income from small niche games; treat that as an anecdote, not an expectation, and aim for a first win rather than a viral hit. Modded/low-effort clone games die quickly.
- Then **play the game for two hours**, not ten minutes: study the core loop, progression (D1/D7 reasons), monetization, bugs and player feedback. If everything is visible in the first hour, players leave.
- Look for twists you can add (a class system for retention in a survival loop, guns, NPC survivors).

## The five checks for a game idea (summary of the viral-factors and checklist videos)
1. **Clear demand signals** on and off Roblox (above). Confirm the fantasy on Creator Exchange: sort by visits for the keyword.
2. **Clear gap in the market / fresh idea.** Many airport games are role-play; the checkpoint/action version was empty. One firefighter game held a steady small audience for years: room to innovate with a trending genre (checkpoint, extraction). A polished FPS that is a plain team deathmatch fails for lack of novelty, while an FPS with building, mystery-box abilities and destruction does well.
3. **Readable mechanics.** Reuse systems already proven by top games so players recognize them (base lock/laser door from Steal a Brain Rot; "buy 1 / buy max" from incremental games; Grow a Garden HUD). Do not copy one-to-one; copy the functioning.
4. **Mobile UX and low friction.** Play on phone/tablet; a cousin playing silently is the best test ([onboarding and UX](onboarding-retention-ux.md)).
5. **Proven-concept mix with roughly 20% change** (Never reuse another creator's names, art, audio, UI, code or thumbnails; adapt mechanics and fantasy only.)  (creator's unvalidated rule of thumb, loosely called the "Pareto principle"): combine two proven concepts for a somewhat fresh result (a Grow-a-Garden-like plot layout + Bedwars floating islands → sky plots separated by bridges, enabling knock-into-the-void).

Also ask: is the idea **clickable** (the "seven-year-old trying to fall asleep" test: what wild imagined scenario does it fulfil? "a hospital run by animals", "toys that are alive", "go to the moon")? Is it relatable or does it have a passionate fan base? Is it shareable/clippable ([game design building blocks](game-design-building-blocks.md))?

## Market phases: blue, red and purple ocean
1. **First to market**: one game creates a fantasy (Fish It, Don't Wake the Brain Rots) and eats the audience.
2. **Saturation**: template sellers and clones flood; most die within weeks to months; the pioneer is hurt too.
3. **Survivors mutate**: new title, thumbnail and a meaningful twist (one "Don't X the Y" game survived the trend with the same loop under a new title and thumbnail; ice fishing, cage fishing, fish tank outlived generic fishing games).
4. **Purple ocean**: high demand but poor execution. Check by typing the fantasy in Creator Exchange; play the best games and ask how to make it 10x better. Underserved *audiences* also count (adult first responders play Emergency Roleplay because nothing else serves them (a second-hand anecdote)).
Entering a brand-new fantasy with no demand is risky; entering a saturated one without mutation produces low CTR.

## Study games to build intuition ("games to watch" method)
Keep a spreadsheet: link, first-play notes (fantasy, tutorial clarity in the first 60-120 s, curiosity loop opened, social flex visibility, progression feel, bugs, mobile friction), and a **hypothesis** ("will reach 3-5K CCU; limited by social flex not visible"). Re-check after one week/two weeks and record whether the hypothesis held and why. Being wrong is still learning. Complements launching your own games; do not stay in theory.

## AI-built games as a niche signal
Games built mostly with AI exist and earn modestly (for example quiz or cleaning-theme games with small but steady estimated earnings). Telltales: generic-looking UI, owner usernames referencing AI tools. Choose genres with low dev time (quiz, clicker, simulator), high demand, low competition and proof that AI-built games already work there. Revenue estimators underreport; treat as rough. Kids tolerate janky polish more than broken or confusing play.

## Adaptation caution and lineage
Adapt mechanics and fantasy only. Do not reuse names, art, audio, code or distinctive expression from the source game; Roblox also ranks near-duplicate metadata lower ([discovery](platform-discovery-metrics.md), [market research](market-research.md)). The creator's examples of adaptation: a Flash blacksmith game became a Roblox forging game, a fishing Flash game became an underwater game, a tower defense classic became a balloon game, a Garry's Mod mode became a hide-and-seek game, a mobile creature game became a pet-themed one. Why reuse works (creator's view): billions of visits have already trained players on proven mechanics, so familiar systems onboard themselves. The creator also advises studying top games intensively (one friend played them about 12 hours a day).
